How do you build a habit that actually sticks?

● Updated August 2026  |  sources cited inline

Direct answer: Pick one or two tiny habits, anchor each to something you already do every day, make the action take under a minute, and expect it to feel automatic in about two months, not three weeks. The research is specific: reaching automaticity took a median of 66 days (range 18, 254) in the UCL study, an occasional miss does not reset you, and stacking a new habit onto an existing one is the most reliable trigger. Track it in a way that takes seconds, and follow the rule "never miss twice."

What the research actually says

The most-cited real-world study is Lally et al. (2010, UCL), which followed people adopting a new daily behaviour. Key findings: automaticity built gradually and plateaued at a median of 66 days, with wide individual variation (18 to 254 days); and missing a single day did not meaningfully break the trajectory. That last point is the evidence behind James Clear's rule "never miss twice", treat one slip as noise, two as a warning. The popular "21 days to a habit" claim is a myth with no support in this data.

The method

  1. Start with 1, 2 habits, not ten. Overloading is the top reason trackers get abandoned.
  2. Make it tiny. "One push-up," "read one page." A habit too small to fail builds the identity; you can scale later.
  3. Stack it onto an anchor. James Clear's formula: "After [current habit], I will [new habit]." The existing habit is the reliable cue.
  4. Log in under 5 seconds. A quick check-off provides the self-monitoring feedback that sustains behaviour. If logging is a chore, you will stop.
  5. Never miss twice. Plan for imperfect weeks; one miss is fine, just don't let it become two.
  6. Give it ~66 days. Judge the system on a two-month horizon, not a two-week one.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it really take to build a habit?

A median of 66 days to reach automaticity, according to the UCL study by Lally et al. (2010), with a wide range of 18 to 254 days depending on the person and behaviour. The common '21 days' figure is a myth unsupported by that data.

What is habit stacking?

Habit stacking links a new habit to an existing one using James Clear's formula: 'After [current habit], I will [new habit].' Your existing daily routine becomes the reliable cue, which is more dependable than motivation or a set time.

Does missing one day ruin a habit?

No. Lally et al. found a single missed day did not meaningfully break the habit-formation trajectory. James Clear summarises this as 'never miss twice', one slip is fine, but avoid making it two in a row.

How many habits should I try to build at once?

One or two. Trying to build many habits simultaneously is a leading reason people abandon the effort. Establish one until it feels automatic, then add the next.
